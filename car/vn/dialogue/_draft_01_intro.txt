# ---- 1. the enlistment office ----
# ART: a fluorescent intake hall; a desk, a recruiter, and a queue of the young and cheap
The first thing I did when they let me walk was try to enlist again.

# [[ the office IS the war: the young queue, the forms, the poor. He's the oldest man in the room. ]]

The recruiter looked at the leg. Then at the file. Then at me.
Two of those three were on the form. The third one he kept looking at anyway, the way you look at something the printer spat out wrong.

"We've got a backlog of whole men," he said, like it was a kindness. "You understand."

I understood. I stood there and understood it for a long time.

# ---- 2. the trip ----
# [[ THE KEY BEAT. He'd have caught himself with the left leg. There is no left leg.
#    Do NOT explain the phantom limb. Let the body do the telling. ]]
# ART: the wide steps down from the intake hall; a man folding in mid-air
On the way out, the step wasn't where the rest of me expected it.
My weight shifted onto a leg that wasn't there - the old reflex, the one the body keeps long after the leg is gone - and it found nothing to stand on.

# [[ phantom limb = the body still believing. Show the fall, never the reason. ]]

I went down the whole flight.

The prosthetic hit first. It always does. That's the part nobody tells you: the machine outlives the piece of you that would have caught it. I lay there a while, cheek on the cold, listening to a joint I paid for grind against a step.

And I thought:

# [[ the tic lands here - the same word from the prologue, now in the gutter. ]]

Good.

Nobody helped me up. That was correct. A man who can't stand in this market is a man the market has already priced.

# ---- 3. the drift ----
# [[ compress. keep the voice. he drinks, fights, does dumb shit. lowest point. ]]
The boom times, when my pops moved out to the Jovian moons as a volunteer for the Giant Leap Pioneer Project, are long over.
The bubble dreams of our fathers popped. The lost children of that era lived as gig-economy freeters, junkies, and burnouts.
Many were gazing bleary-eyed at the end of a gunbarrel - until the invasion of Io.
It doesn't matter how good or bad things get - even beneath the all-seeing red eye of Jupiter, everyone's gotta make a buck. Or find a fight.

I don't remember which bar the first one was in. I remember the second, and that I won it, and that winning felt like the only thing that had felt like anything in a year.

# [[ "the only thing that had felt like anything" = he's alive only in violence. that's the crack the war left. ]]

A broken soldier in a dead market is a commodity with no buyers. I found the gutter the way you find anything down there: by falling into it.

I crossed her again a week later, in the cut behind the fuel exchange. She was running the bit again - louder now, confident. She'd learned that it *worked*.

It didn't.

The one in front slapped the phone out of her hand. I heard the plastic skitter across the grate. Somebody behind her took her arm.
The one who'd slapped it picked the phone up, turned the camera around, and started reading the numbers out loud. Playing host.

I don't remember deciding. I remember three shots, and the three of them, and the cane for the one who turned toward me with his hands up - as if hands ever helped anyone.

# ---- the turn ----
# [[ his lecture FAILS - but the failure is the point. ]]
I told her what I'd wanted to tell her at the bar. That the bit doesn't work. That it only ever works when there are people near enough to care enough to stop - and out here there never are. That she'd been lucky exactly once, and me being that luck wasn't a plan.

She kept her eyes down while I said it. Nodded, small. Demure, even.

And then she looked up, and she wasn't demure at all.

Chip: It did work, though. Didn't it.

I didn't have an answer for that.

# [[ THE MUSE - his real reason, under the stated one. Keep the dismissal in it:
#    "airhead" is HIS misread, the same one the reader will later eat. ]]
She was an airhead. Pink-haired, too loud, a thousand light-years from anywhere she belonged, and she was going to get herself killed and I was going to watch it happen because I was tired.

That's what I told myself.

But she did something to a room that I couldn't name, and I don't think she knew she was doing it. Around her, people forgot. They forgot the front. They forgot the units, the shelling, what was in the rubble. They remembered that they were people, and that there were other people, and that those were two separate things a person could get back.

She made the war stop being the only thing in the room.
